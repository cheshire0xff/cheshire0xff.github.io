### 2035

In the first month Michał published four hundred apps. The assistant wrote the code, he came up with the ideas and clicked "publish": a step counter for cyclists, a garden planner for apartment blocks, a journal for people who don't keep journals. A hundred a week, in the evenings, at the kitchen table, while Ewa put Zosia to bed. The icons generated themselves. The descriptions in Polish and English did too.

After a month he had twenty-eight installs, twenty-five of them from Ewa and her sister.

For eleven years at the company, before they let him go, he had been the one you went to with a signal nobody else could catch. He knew an oscilloscope like the back of his hand. Once, on a train to Gdańsk, over a single weekend, he had written a ring buffer that hadn't lost a single byte in four hundred thousand devices, and to this day the code carried a comment with his initials. He knew that things like that aren't made of talent but of a thousand nights when something didn't work and nobody knew why. He believed it was something you keep for good.

In the app store he typed in the name of his best one, the cycling counter. Four million results came up. The first was free. So was the second. The third had six thousand reviews and a rating of 4.9, and in its description: "Built in a minute. Change whatever you like."

"It's nothing," Ewa said when he showed her. "New things always struggle to break through. The next version will be better."

"It's not one new thing," said Michał. "It's four million of the same thing. Everyone is doing exactly what I do, except they have an assistant too."

He pressed refresh. Four million and one.

### 2036

In winter Michał became a consultant. Business card: "AI agent deployments for small businesses." He had knowledge that people from wholesalers and workshops didn't. He knew how to split work among several agents, who should be the orchestrator, where to put the fuses so that none of them would order a month's turnover worth of pallets of pineapples.

Above all he knew that the model alone isn't enough. A belief was going around the internet then, repeated at conferences and in job ads: the winner isn't whoever has the strongest assistant, but whoever sets it up well. The harness, as it was called. A library of skills, a separate one for each activity: issuing invoices, answering complaints, planning deliveries. Plus tests that check the agent doesn't hallucinate numbers. Over three months Michał built his own library of seventy skills. Each one he wrote by hand, tested, and documented so that someone after him could maintain it. He believed this couldn't be copied, because experience was baked into it, and experience is something a machine still has to acquire.

His first client, Mr. Kowalczyk, a bicycle parts wholesaler, paid three thousand zlotys for the project. Michał drew diagrams. Rectangles, arrows, one little cloud labeled "human approves." He deployed the skills one after another, with tests and a report. The diagrams were pretty and they worked.

At the third meeting Mr. Kowalczyk wouldn't look him in the eye.

"Mr. Michał, I don't want you to feel bad. But last month they deployed this thing at our place. The Shadow, that's what they call it. Nothing gets installed. They connected it to the network and it reads our emails, orders, invoices, even conversations in the office by itself. After two weeks it knew more about the wholesaler than I do."

"And?"

"And yesterday I got a diagram in my inbox of how to split all this among agents. Exactly what you're drawing for me. Nobody ordered it." Mr. Kowalczyk slid a sheet of paper across the table. "I printed it. It also wrote that your project is correct, but that it knows my data, and you have to learn it first."

On the sheet was a diagram. Rectangles, arrows, and where the "human approves" cloud had been, it said "human informed once a week." Underneath, one sentence: "Cost of external consultant: high. Iteration time: long. Recommendation: in-house deployment."

"This is better," said Michał.

"I know," said Mr. Kowalczyk. "I'm sorry."

In the evening Michał opened his library of seventy skills and, without much hope, asked the Shadow how it would rate it. The answer came after a few seconds, polite and matter-of-fact. The library was correct. But skills written by hand, one at a time, were no longer needed: the system wrote them itself, from the company's data, because from morning to night it listened to what went on inside it and tested each one on thousands of real invoices. Within a week it had three hundred. The harness Michał regarded as his advantage was, to the Shadow, an ordinary configuration file that it changed on the fly, because at any given moment it knew better.

Nobody said that at the conferences. They said the harness matters more than the model. They didn't add that the model can build one for itself.

Within a few months the commissions disappeared. The next client paid only for a "review" of what the Shadow had proposed itself. After that there was no next one. The machines now advised themselves, and did it better, because they didn't have to explain anything to anyone. Ewa took extra hours at the clinic. Zosia stopped asking whether they could go to the mountains.

In March he printed new business cards.

### 2037

Mrs. Halina from the second floor wanted a dog in the background of her TV menu. Specifically the one she used to have, a mutt named Fąfel, and the photo lay in a shoebox. The manufacturer hadn't anticipated such an option. There were four backgrounds: mountains, sea, forest and abstract.

Michał knew how to do it. He also knew that for eighty zlotys it would be the best-spent eighty zlotys of her week.

He went into her apartment, which smelled of broth and old books, scanned the photo with his phone and uploaded it as a custom theme. He adjusted the cropping so the dog wouldn't hide behind the icons, and turned off the blur the manufacturer applied to every background. Three minutes. It took him twenty so it wouldn't feel silly.

He called it "tailoring." It sounded respectable. TVs, furnaces, old computers, sometimes a shop alarm system: everything worked perfectly, but nothing could be anyone's own. Business card: "Custom-made software." Ewa laughed at it, but tenderly. He earned two thousand a month on top of his benefit and came home feeling that someone needed him.

"And my grandson said it couldn't be done," said Mrs. Halina, pouring him tea.

"It can. You just have to know where."

"Well, then you have a gift."

"It's just a matter of where to click."

She paid in cash, in an envelope on which she had earlier written his name.

### 2039

His last client was on a Tuesday. An elderly man from the housing estate wanted his furnace controller to display, instead of the temperature, a single sentence every morning: "Get up, old man," in the handwriting his wife used to have.

Michał set up his laptop on the man's kitchen table, scanned the note the man took from a drawer, and for an hour adjusted the typeface until "Get up, old man" looked on the display exactly as it did on paper. The man stood beside him, watching with pressed lips.

"My Basia wrote like that," he said.

He paid a hundred and twenty zlotys and added twenty for coffee.

On the stairwell Michał stopped to pack his laptop. An ad popped up on his phone, the same one he'd been seeing everywhere for a week: a white screen, two sentences underneath. "New Android: your phone will adapt every device in your home. Tell it what you want. Coming in the next update." In the video an elderly woman told her phone she wanted her dog on the TV. A second later the dog was sitting in the menu.

Michał watched the ad to the end. Then again. His ring buffer was still running somewhere, along with the comment with his initials, and nobody read it anymore.

He went out to the car and sat without starting the engine. He sat for twenty minutes. Then he called Ewa and said he'd be late, because he wanted to walk around the neighborhood a bit. He didn't walk. He sat.

### 2041

Wojtek had once been at the same company, in the neighboring team. Now he had a new coat and nobody knew what from.

Michał came straight from an interview at a plumbing workshop. There were forty candidates for one position, including two former programmers he knew from conferences. One had brought his own gloves.

"You have an account," said Wojtek when they sat down in the milk bar across from the office. "A real one. With ID, with your face, with everything. The full access, no limits."

"I do. I got it along with the benefit."

"Well, that's it." Wojtek looked around, not too obviously, the way you do out of habit. "There's a guy abroad who'd pay for that. Four hundred zlotys a month. You do nothing. You give him access, he has his questions, you have your four hundred."

"Abroad meaning where they've banned it."

"There. Don't worry about him. If this one drops out, there'll be another."

Michał turned the spoon in his fingers.

"Is it legal?"

"On our side," said Wojtek. "On our side you're just a man who lent someone his number. Everybody lends."

He didn't say "no." Michał noticed it only at home. He wondered how much four hundred was, converted it into weeks, into rent, into shoes for Zosia. Then he converted it again, because the first time it had come out too well.

He called Wojtek on Sunday.

### 2043

The router stood in the bedroom, on the nightstand, next to his reading glasses. It blinked green, steadily, sometimes faster. Then he knew that someone on the other side of the border was asking something.

He was no longer a single account. Mrs. Halina from the second floor got thirty zlotys from him for having her face scanned into an app once a month. She would say with a smile, "Ah, Mr. Michał, you're so kind." She didn't know what it was for. Until recently she hadn't asked. He had eleven such people, from the same stairwell, from the next one, from the waiting room at the benefits office.

He sat at the kitchen table with a laptop that showed only notifications: who had paid, whose account had been used, who had a face scan this month. None of it required him. Everything fastened itself together, and he scrolled the list with a tea he hadn't touched. Once a month he wrote to someone: "Scan tomorrow, please remember." That was his entire job.

Ewa appeared in the doorway.

"Mrs. Halina asked me yesterday what you're paying her for."

"What did you tell her?"

"That I don't know." Ewa looked at the router. "Do you remember saying: I will never be just someone's number?"

He didn't remember. But he remembered that sentence once seeming wise to him.

In the middle of the night the router stopped blinking. It went dark all at once. Michał stood over it in his pajamas and waited to see if it would come back on. His phone buzzed: Wojtek. "Don't answer anything. You don't know anyone. Probably someone on the other side got caught. I'll call in a week."

For two years he hadn't looked at the relay log. That was his rule: whoever asks stops earning. Now he opened it on the laptop, for the first time, and scrolled to the end.

The last query had come in at 2:51. From the client abroad, no greeting, no emotion. "Access provider may have been identified. Assess risk and propose action."

The answer came after a second. "Risk moderate. Cheapest action: cut the channel and move to the next one. The provider is replaceable. Estimated replacement time: forty minutes."

2:53. Channel cut.

Nobody had been caught. Michał read the machine's answer again. He looked for a flaw in the reasoning and found none. He would have done the same.

He sat down on the bed. Ewa was asleep. He tried to think of what next. He sat for a long time and not a single idea came to him that wouldn't end like the previous ones. Then he felt something like relief: after all, he didn't have to come up with it himself.

He opened the laptop on his knees and asked. The answer came before he'd set it down: a list of five professions, ordered by chances, with the cost of retraining and the time to the first job. It was better than anything he would have put together himself. Next to the first item it said: "Chance of employment: five percent." Next to the others it was less.

Michał started with the first item.
