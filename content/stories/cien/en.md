### Four weeks

Olek came to work first, as always, except that now there was no one left to notice.

The open space on the third floor had forty desks. Eleven were occupied. The rest stood under the same lamps, with the same mugs nobody had taken away, because why would they. By the elevator, next to the fire extinguisher, a small green light was on. Nobody remembered when it had been installed. Nobody asked what it meant.

He sat down at his desk and opened a new ticket. Migrate the controller firmware to the new MCU: extract the hardware layer, separate the logic from the HAL. He wrote the description, then the acceptance criteria, one after another. They were clear and complete, exactly the kind he had been writing for ten years. Nobody required it of him. Nobody required anything of anyone anymore.

Through the glass wall he could see the lab. At the station where their boards used to sit, a kid in an agency vest sat with a tablet propped against the monitor. He came in for a few hours a day. He plugged in the programmer, connected the next board, pressed reset when the tablet told him to, and took a photo of the oscilloscope screen. He didn't know what was on those boards, and he didn't have to. Something told him what to connect, and he connected it. In his head Olek called him the hands. Not with contempt. It was simply the only part of him that was still needed.

At nine fifteen he gathered the team around the table in the conference room. There were eight chairs, and four people sitting. Two of them never said a word: one sat with his hands behind his head, the other held his mug in both hands and stared out the window, where nothing was happening. Igor, who used to sit in the seat next to them, wasn't there. He had been upstairs for a week, in meetings.

Olek went through the tasks as he did every day, and at the end he added:

"One more thing. The controller migration to the new MCU. Kuba, can you take it?"

Kuba looked up. Twenty-three years old, first year on the job, the only one on the team who still read documentation for pleasure.

"Sure."

"The criteria are in the ticket."

The one with his hands behind his head laughed briefly, without joy, but said nothing. Olek pretended not to hear.

"That's all. Thank you."

They stood up. The two older ones left without a word. The one with the mug washed it in the kitchen and put it on the rack, in a row with the mugs of those who no longer came.

Kuba stayed at the table.

"Can I ask something?"

"Go ahead."

Kuba moved his fingers on the keyboard as if searching it for words.

"This task. Does it... still make sense?"

Olek looked at him. He remembered being like that himself once. He remembered it very precisely, and that was exactly why he couldn't lie.

"The sprint runs until the end of the month," he said.

"I know. But I'm asking whether..."

"I know what you're asking."

In the corridor the elevator doors closed. The green light kept shining, steady, without a blink.

"Do it well," Olek said. "Then it will make sense, at least to you."

Kuba nodded. He looked like someone who had heard an answer that was true but not the one he'd asked for. He picked up his laptop and went to his desk.

Olek stayed alone in the room. He went back to the ticket and read his own acceptance criteria once more. They were good.

He added one more: behavior on power loss during a write to flash. He wondered why, since nobody would check it, and added it anyway.

### Three weeks

Igor couldn't keep quiet. Olek had known it for five years and it had always pleased him: a minute in the kitchen was enough to learn what Igor had eaten for lunch, what his mother-in-law had said, and why the coffee machine, the same one for nine years, was evil incarnate. In this company he was like a radio nobody had switched on that played anyway.

Today he was playing too. He stood at the coffee machine with his back to the door, talking before Olek could say a word.

"...and I tell him it's not about the pressure, it's the lady from administration who hasn't changed the filter in three years. And he says the filter doesn't matter, because the coffee is bad anyway. So I ask: then why do you drink it?" He turned around. "Oh, there you are. Coffee?"

"Please."

Igor pressed the button. The machine growled. For a moment neither of them said anything, and it was so unusual that they both noticed.

"I've got a new position," Igor said cheerfully. "They gave it to me upstairs. Continuity Custodian. Sounds like a priest for cats."

"Congratulations."

"Don't congratulate me." He laughed, but too briefly. "One for the whole department. I'm supposed to sit and watch whether anything breaks. And if it breaks, then..."

"Then what?"

"I don't know. They didn't say."

The machine finished. Igor handed him a mug, and then, as if remembering something, lowered his voice.

"My brother-in-law in Wrocław," he said, almost whispering. "Their company rolled it out in January. Two weeks and it was empty. The whole open space. One guy left, and the cleaning lady." He broke off, looked around the empty kitchen and laughed at himself. "Look at me. Whispering. As if it could hear."

"It can," said Olek.

"Exactly. Everyone's like that. Downstairs they call it the Shadow. Because it's everywhere and nobody has seen it."

"Shadow."

"Yeah." Igor scratched the back of his neck. "Listen. If it were up to me..."

"Igor."

"No, I want to say that if I could, I would..."

The machine, which Igor had started for himself, spat the last drops into the mug and fell silent.

"It still works," said Olek.

Igor looked at the machine. For a moment they both looked at it.

Then Igor told one more story about his mother-in-law, a long one, completely made up, and Olek laughed in all the right places.

### Two weeks

Rafał stopped writing code on Monday. Nobody announced it, but Olek saw it from the other end of the room: hands resting on the keyboard and not moving, eyes fixed on a screen that showed nothing.

By eleven he was standing at Olek's desk. He didn't wait for him to look up.

"Two weeks. Two. And you're still assigning tasks."

"That's my job."

"It was."

Olek didn't answer.

"Remember what they wrote?" Rafał pulled out his phone, but he didn't need to look. "'No action is required on your part.' Do you understand that sentence? It's not information. It's a diagnosis. We aren't needed for anything. Not even for pretending to work."

"I know."

"Yeah? Then why do you still..."

"Because I don't know how to do it any other way," said Olek.

Rafał looked at him for a moment. He was angry, but beneath the anger was something he couldn't hide, and that was worse.

"I wrote that linker script," he said more quietly. "For the new MCU. Kuba started with the documentation, and I took the script myself. Three days. I enjoyed writing it, for the first time in a year." He snorted. "Do you know how stupid that sounds? First time in a year I liked something, and I know nobody will open it."

"I'll open it."

"You." Rafał shook his head. "Great."

He went back to his desk. Olek watched him unplug his keyboard, his own mechanical one, the one he'd bought with his first paycheck, coil the cable and slide it into his backpack. He didn't look at anyone. All you could hear was the zipper.

He left before noon and nobody stopped him. On the desk remained a bright rectangle in the dust, where the keyboard had stood.

In the afternoon Henryk brought a cake.

He set it on the kitchen table, in a cardboard bakery box, and put a plastic knife beside it. He didn't say why. Olek, who had come in for coffee, stopped in the doorway.

"What's the occasion?"

"Twenty-two years," said Henryk. "Today. I brought one on the first day too."

"I didn't know."

"Nobody did. That's why the cake." He cut a slice. His hand didn't shake. "You're all still struggling. I made my peace with it a year ago, before you noticed. That's why I can cut."

Kuba came in a moment later, saw the cake and stopped. Henryk silently cut him a slice and handed it over on a paper plate.

They ate, the three of them, without talking. The cake was good. Behind the wall, by the elevator, the green light was on.

### One week

In the last week people stopped writing to each other in chat. Olek noticed it on Tuesday: the team channel had been silent since morning, and there was more talking in the corridor than in the whole previous year. Quietly, in doorways, with mugs in their hands. About nothing important. About kids, about renovations, about who was going where on vacation.

Rafał wasn't there. His desk stood empty, with the rectangle in the dust that nobody had wiped away.

On Wednesday morning Olek opened the review queue and saw a pull request nobody had submitted. Title: "Migrate hardware layer to the new MCU". The task he had assigned to Kuba in the first week. Kuba hadn't even started it. The ticket history held nothing but Kuba's assignment from a month ago, no comments. The task had simply changed status.

Author: the service account, the same one that for a month had been closing small tickets at night, in other departments, while in the morning everyone pretended it was meant to be that way. The Shadow. Status: ready for review. Created: yesterday, 02:14.

Nobody had asked it to. It saw an open task and did it, the way someone walking past an unlit lamp switches it on because it's dark.

Olek did what he always did. He started with the description, then the tests, then the diff, top to bottom. The logic separated from the HAL, just as he wanted, with an interface thin as a sheet of paper. The interrupt table moved and documented. The linker script, the same one Rafał had spent three days on, only shorter. Olek opened Rafał's file next to it to compare. The difference was in a comment explaining why the bootloader section lay where it did.

He looked for something to comment on. Not out of politeness. That was his job: every review ended with at least one question.

In the description he found the list of acceptance criteria. His own, the ones from the ticket, including the last, the one about power loss during a flash write, which he had added on the first day for no clear reason. Each had its proof attached. Next to the last: "Verified on the board."

On the board. Olek leaned back from the monitor.

In the corner of the room, at a free station, sat the kid from the lab: thin, with a tablet and an agency lanyard. Since Monday he had been moved closer, so he wouldn't walk the corridor. He was connecting another board to the rig. He didn't look at the code, didn't look at anyone. A line appeared on the tablet, he nodded, plugged in the cable, waited a dozen seconds, unplugged it. Then the next one.

The hands. Someone had once decided that this was a role a human could still fill. Olek couldn't remember who.

He went back to the review. He read the diff a second time, more slowly. Not because he suspected anything. He just didn't want to approve it yet.

There was nothing. There wasn't even a typo in a comment.

Kuba stood behind his back.

"That was mine," he said. He wasn't asking.

"It was."

"It would have taken me a week." Kuba looked at the screen. "Is it good?"

Olek could have said many things. That first versions always have bugs. That it must be checked on real hardware. It had been checked on real hardware.

"Better than I would have written," he said.

Kuba was silent for a moment. Then he patted him on the shoulder, awkwardly, like someone who had never done it before, and went to the kitchen.

Olek stayed with the open review. The cursor blinked in the comment field. The field was empty and was going to stay that way.

### The last day

Olek spent two days writing the handover document. Forty pages: architecture, decisions, why this was left this way and not that, which modules are fragile, whom to ask about what. He wrote it carefully, with examples, with a list of things to check before anyone touched the power supply.

He knew nobody would read it. Not because Igor was lazy. The Shadow didn't need a document, because it saw every commit, every conversation and every version that had ever passed through the network. It knew more about that code than Olek did.

He saved the file and sent it to Igor. The reply came in a minute: "Thanks, great". Olek decided it was an honest answer.

The team said goodbye one by one, though they were all leaving the same day, the way people say goodbye at a bus stop when they don't know if they'll see each other again. Henryk shook his hand and held it a moment longer than was proper.

"Good that it was you," he said.

"What?"

"Good that you were the lead." He let go of his hand. "That's all."

Kuba stood nearby and didn't know what to do with his hands. Olek wrote his phone number on a piece of paper, though Kuba already had it.

"Call me when you're writing something," he said. "Not for work. For yourself."

"Okay."

"Really. I'll always be glad to read it."

Kuba nodded and said nothing, because his voice would have cracked.

Then Olek sat down at his desk and opened the review. The cursor was still blinking in the empty field. He typed four letters.

LGTM.

He approved. The button changed color, the status changed to "merged", and something that had been his for a month disappeared from the task list. He felt nothing in particular. Somewhere in the server room, or in the cloud, or everywhere at once, the Shadow accepted his signature the same way it accepted all the others.

Igor was waiting by the elevator. He held the cardboard box in which Olek had packed his things: a mug, a charger, two books he had never returned to the shelf. Someone had brought them from his desk while Olek was talking to Kuba.

"Procedure," said Igor, and held out his hand for the access card.

Olek took it off the lanyard. The card was warm from his body.

"It was good working with you," said Igor. He stumbled. "Is. Was. I don't know how you say it."

"Was," said Olek. "It's fine."

The elevator arrived. The green light shone beside it, steady, without a blink.

"Igor," said Olek, already in the doorway. "The coffee machine needs descaling. Once a quarter. Nobody remembers."

"I'll write it down."

"Don't write it down." Olek smiled. "Just remember."

The doors closed. There was a mirror in the elevator, and in it a man with a box. He looked tired.

Downstairs it was raining. Olek put the box on his head like a little roof and walked to the bus stop.

The bus was due in seven minutes. His phone buzzed in his pocket. His company account was still active until midnight, so the automation sent him what it sent everyone with repository permissions.

"Repository configuration change. Parameter: human approval required. Previous value: enabled. New value: disabled. 16:42."

He looked at the time his approval had been sent. 16:41.

He read the message again, more slowly. It had no author and no reason. Nobody had waited for his signature to switch that requirement off. It was enough that he had been the last one to give it.

The rain drummed on the cardboard. The bus pulled in, the doors opened. Olek got on and paid for his ticket, like someone who still had something to pay with.
